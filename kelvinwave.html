What happened

    What is built on real data.

    • Tides: daily highest water at La Jolla (NOAA station 9410230) for two years from August 1 of 1982, 1997, 2015 and 2026, computed from NOAA's published harmonic constituents. A spot check against NOAA's own predictions for December 21 to 26, 2026 agreed within 4 cm.
    • Flood levels: NOAA's minor (136 cm) and moderate (164 cm) coastal flood thresholds for La Jolla, in centimeters above mean sea level (1983 to 2001 epoch).
    • Observed monthly values: NOAA tide gauge monthly mean sea level and monthly highest water at La Jolla. The anomaly shown is the monthly mean minus the mean of the predicted tide, so it includes long-term sea level rise since the 1983 to 2001 epoch.
    • Observed daily values for 2026: GLORYS analysis and forecast from Dillon Amaya's coastal trapped wave page, October 20 to December 3, 2026, measured against a 1993 to 2025 average.
    • Wave speed: the default of 2.7 m/s matches the phase speed reported on the same page. Wave size is tuned to the 15 to 30 cm (6 to 12 inches) range reported there.

    What is simplified. The ocean is a two-layer model: wave speed is the square root of reduced gravity times warm layer thickness, and each centimeter of surface rise goes with about 2 m of thermocline deepening. Weaker trade winds raise the east and lower the west; the change travels east at Kelvin wave speed and west three times more slowly, as Rossby waves do. The size of that response is tuned so trade winds at 30% of normal give about +35 cm in the eastern equatorial Pacific, close to the +35 to +45 cm in the GLORYS analysis for October and November 2026. Storms add a fixed 20 cm for about two days. El Niño is officially defined by sea surface temperature, which this model does not calculate. Reflected waves, seasons, long-term sea level rise and local weather are left out.
